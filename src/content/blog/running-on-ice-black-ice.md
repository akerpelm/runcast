Running on ice is safest when you avoid it in the first place: black ice forms when wet surfaces refreeze, when freezing rain falls, or when fog freezes on contact, and it's almost invisible. If you can't avoid it, use traction devices, shorten your stride, and slow down on bridges, shaded paths and anywhere water collects.

Ice causes more winter running injuries than cold does. The good news is that it follows predictable patterns, and once you know them you can see the risk coming before you step outside.

## Why Black Ice Is So Dangerous

Black ice isn't actually black. It's a thin, clear layer that lets the dark pavement show through, so the ground just looks wet. You often don't know it's there until your foot slides.

It's worst in the hours after a freeze, especially early in the morning, when surfaces have had all night to lose heat.

## The Six Ice Scenarios

[Should I Run](/) checks six scenarios every hour. Risk only goes up as more of them apply, and the final rating is the worst one triggered.

### 1. Freezing Rain, Sleet or Snow

If precipitation is falling at 34°F or below, ice can form on contact. Freezing rain and sleet are rated **HIGH**. Snow is **MODERATE**, because it reduces traction and can hide ice underneath.

### 2. Black Ice After a Thaw

If it was above 34°F at some point in the last 24 hours and it's now 32°F or below, any water on the ground is probably refreezing. If there was rain or other precipitation in that time, the risk is **HIGH**. If it was dry but the high was above 40°F, melted snow and puddles are likely to refreeze, so the risk is **MODERATE**. This is the classic freeze-thaw trap.

### 3. Frost

On clear (cloud cover under 40%), calm (wind under 10 mph) nights between 28°F and 38°F, when the dew point is within 5°F of the temperature, frost forms on surfaces. It's rated **LOW**, but it makes wooden boardwalks, metal grates and painted lines slick.

### 4. Freezing Fog

When it's 33°F or below, the dew point is within 2°F of the temperature, and wind is under 8 mph, fog droplets can freeze onto every surface they touch. This one is rated **HIGH** because it can coat everything at once.

### 5. Bridges and Overpasses

Between 30°F and 36°F, the app adds a note that bridges and overpasses may be icy even when roads are clear. They have cold air above and below, so they lose heat faster than the ground. This note doesn't raise the risk rating, but take it seriously.

### 6. Early Morning After a Clear Night

At 40°F or below with cloud cover under 30%, early morning runs get a **LOW** rating. On a clear night the ground radiates heat into the sky and can be colder than the air, so you can find ice even when the thermometer says it's above freezing.

## Where Ice Hides

Even on a low-risk day, some spots stay icy:

- **Bridges, overpasses and boardwalks**
- **Shaded stretches** under trees, beside buildings and on north-facing slopes, where the sun never melts the overnight ice
- **Low points and dips** where water pools
- **Downspouts and driveway edges** where meltwater runs across the sidewalk
- **Metal grates, manhole covers and painted road lines**
- **Curb cuts and crosswalks**, where slush gets packed down

If a surface looks wet and the temperature is near or below freezing, assume it's ice.

## Traction Devices

For regular winter running, traction is worth buying:

- **Slip-on traction devices** stretch over your shoes and use coils or small spikes to grip ice. They're easy to put on and take off, but can feel awkward on bare pavement.
- **Trail shoes** with deep lugs help on packed snow, though they don't do much on clear ice.
- **Studded running shoes** or shoes with screws added to the outsole give the most grip on ice-covered routes.

Whatever you use, try it on a short, safe loop first so you know how it feels.

## Change How You Run

On slick ground, your form matters as much as your shoes:

1. **Shorten your stride.** Keep your feet under your hips instead of reaching forward. Long strides land on the heel, which is where slips start.
2. **Increase your cadence.** Quick, light steps keep you balanced.
3. **Land flat.** Aim for your whole foot to touch down rather than striking with the heel.
4. **Slow down.** Treat icy days as easy days. This is not the time for speed work.
5. **Keep your hands free.** Don't run with hands in pockets. You'll want them for balance.
6. **Take turns wide and slow.** Most slips happen when you change direction.

Run in daylight where possible, choose routes that get cleared or salted early, and favor loops close to home.

## FAQ

### Is it safe to run on black ice?

Not really. If you know there's widespread black ice, the safest choice is a treadmill or a later start once the sun has had time to work. If you must go, wear traction and run slowly.

### Can there be ice when it's above freezing?

Yes. After a clear night, the ground can be colder than the air, so frost and ice patches can be present even when the air temperature is a few degrees above 32°F.

### What time of day is ice worst?

Usually early morning, after a night of cooling and before the sun reaches the ground. Midday runs on sunny days tend to have the best footing.

### Do I need traction devices if I only see a little ice?

A few patches can be stepped around. If your route has lots of shaded or untreated stretches, traction is worth it.

## Bottom Line

Black ice is predictable: watch for refreezing after a thaw, freezing rain, freezing fog, and clear, cold mornings. Slow down on bridges, in shade and over metal.
