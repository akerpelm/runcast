A headwind slows you down by about 1.2 seconds per mile for every mph of wind, so a 15 mph headwind costs roughly 18 seconds per mile. A tailwind gives back only about half of that, which is why running in the wind almost always makes a run slower overall.

Wind is easy to ignore when you check the forecast, but it affects your pace, your effort, how cold you feel and what you should wear. Here's how to plan for it.

## How Much Does Wind Slow You Down?

[Should I Run](/) estimates wind's effect on pace with a simple rule: about **1.2 seconds per mile per mph of headwind**. Here's what that looks like for a runner with an 8:00/mile pace:

| Wind Speed | Headwind Cost | Pace Into Wind | Tailwind Benefit | Pace With Wind |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 10 mph | +12 s/mile | 8:12 | -6 s/mile | 7:54 |
| 15 mph | +18 s/mile | 8:18 | -9 s/mile | 7:51 |
| 20 mph | +24 s/mile | 8:24 | -12 s/mile | 7:48 |

These are estimates for wind blowing straight at you or straight behind you. A crosswind falls somewhere in between, and gusty wind feels harder than a steady one because you keep adjusting your balance.

The bigger point is that you shouldn't fight it. Running a headwind mile at your normal pace means running it at a harder effort. Run by effort and let the watch show a slower number.

## Why the Tailwind Doesn't Pay It Back

You'd expect an out-and-back to even out: lose time going one way, win it back the other. It doesn't.

Air resistance grows quickly with the speed of the air hitting you. Into a headwind, the air hits you at your running speed *plus* the wind speed, and the drag climbs steeply. With a tailwind, the wind first cancels out the breeze you create by running. It only starts pushing you once it's faster than you are. So the help is always smaller than the cost.

The app models this as the tailwind giving back about half of the headwind cost. On a 6-mile out-and-back with 3 miles each way:

| Wind | Lost Going Out | Gained Coming Back | Net Cost |
|---|---|---|---|
| 10 mph | 36 s | 18 s | About 18 s slower |
| 15 mph | 54 s | 27 s | About 27 s slower |
| 20 mph | 72 s | 36 s | About 36 s slower |

That's a modest loss on an easy run. On a race or a goal workout, it's enough to matter.

## Route Strategy: Out Into the Wind

If you can choose your route, **start into the wind and come home with it behind you**. There are three good reasons:

1. **You're freshest at the start.** It's easier to handle the hardest part of the run before you're tired.
2. **You'll be sweaty on the way back.** Damp clothing loses heat fast in wind. Having the wind at your back for the second half keeps you much warmer.
3. **You won't get stuck.** If the wind picks up, you're not facing a long, cold slog into it at the end.

A few more tips:

- **Use shelter.** Tree-lined paths, streets between buildings and valleys block a lot of wind. Open waterfronts, bridges and fields don't.
- **Run loops in strong wind.** Shorter loops spread the headwind into smaller pieces.
- **Run in a group.** Taking turns at the front of a group is an old racing tactic, and it works on training runs too.
- **Shift hard workouts.** Gusts above 30 mph push the app toward an easy day. Strong wind makes intervals and tempo runs hard to pace, so save them for a calmer day.

Should I Run also counts wind and gusts when it scores the [best time to run](/blog/best-time-to-run-summer/). Wind often drops in the early morning or late evening, so moving your run by an hour or two can help.

## Wind Chill: The Cold Side of Wind

Wind doesn't just slow you down. It makes you colder. The NWS wind chill formula estimates how cold it feels on exposed skin, and it applies when the temperature is 50°F or below and wind is above 3 mph.

| Air Temp | 10 mph | 15 mph | 20 mph |
|---|---|---|---|
| 40°F | 34°F | 32°F | 30°F |
| 30°F | 21°F | 19°F | 17°F |
| 20°F | 9°F | 6°F | 4°F |

Two things to remember:

1. **Running into the wind adds your own speed** to what your face feels. The headwind leg will always be the coldest part of the run.
2. **Wind chill drives the cold pace adjustment.** At a wind chill of 20–29°F, the app suggests 2–4% slower. Combined with headwind cost, a windy winter day can slow you down noticeably. See [is it too cold to run](/blog/is-it-too-cold-to-run/) for the full table.

## What to Wear in Wind

Because Should I Run dresses you for the feels-like temperature, wind usually means one more layer. When wind is above 15 mph, the app also adds a **wind-resistant layer**. A light shell over your normal layers blocks a lot of wind without trapping much heat.

Cover the areas that take the most wind: ears, hands and the front of your body. A vest with a wind-blocking front is a good choice when your legs are fine but your chest gets cold. ## FAQ

### Is it harder to run into the wind?

Yes. At the same pace, a headwind raises your effort. At the same effort, it slows you by about 1.2 seconds per mile per mph.

### Does a tailwind make you faster?

A little. It gives back about half of what the same headwind costs, so a run with both directions still ends up slower.

### How windy is too windy to run?

Strong, steady wind is mostly a pace problem. Gusts above 30 mph push Should I Run toward an easy day, and very strong gusts can bring down branches, so avoid tree-lined routes on those days.

### Should I adjust my pace goals on windy days?

Yes. Run headwind sections by effort and accept the slower numbers. Your average pace will be slower than on a calm day, and that's expected.

## Bottom Line

Wind costs about 1.2 seconds per mile per mph of headwind and gives back only about half that when it's behind you. Start into the wind, finish with it at your back, use sheltered routes, and add a wind-resistant layer above 15 mph.
