For most healthy runners, it's rarely too cold to run as long as you dress for it, but once the wind chill drops below 0°F it's time to seriously consider the treadmill. The number to watch is wind chill, not air temperature, because wind is what turns a cold run into a frostbite risk.

Cold is easier to manage than heat. You can add layers, but you can't take off more than everything. Still, there are real limits, and they come from wind, exposed skin and footing.

## Wind Chill Is the Number That Matters

Wind chill estimates how cold the air feels on exposed skin once wind is pulling heat away. The National Weather Service formula applies when the temperature is 50°F or below and wind is above 3 mph.

The effect is bigger than most people expect:

| Air Temp | 5 mph | 10 mph | 15 mph | 20 mph |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 30°F | 25°F | 21°F | 19°F | 17°F |
| 20°F | 13°F | 9°F | 6°F | 4°F |
| 10°F | 1°F | -4°F | -7°F | -9°F |
| 0°F | -11°F | -16°F | -19°F | -22°F |

A 10°F morning with a 15 mph wind is effectively a -7°F morning on your face. That's the number [Should I Run](/) uses for cold pace adjustment and treadmill warnings.

Keep in mind that running creates its own wind. Heading into a breeze at running speed makes exposed skin colder than the forecast suggests. For more, see [running in the wind](/blog/running-in-wind/).

## How Cold Slows You Down

Cold affects pace through heavier clothing, stiffer muscles, cautious footing and the work of keeping warm. Should I Run adjusts your pace by wind chill:

| Wind Chill | Pace Adjustment |
|---|---|
| 40°F and above | 0% |
| 30–39°F | 0–2% slower |
| 20–29°F | 2–4% slower |
| 10–19°F | 4–6% slower |
| 0–9°F | 6–8% slower |
| Below 0°F | 8%+ slower, with an "Extreme cold, consider treadmill" warning |

**Example:** your easy pace is 9:00/mile and the wind chill is 15°F. A 4–6% adjustment puts you at roughly 9:22–9:32/mile. That's normal. Running slower in the cold isn't a fitness problem, so don't chase your summer splits.

## When to Choose the Treadmill

Cold alone is rarely the reason to stay inside. These are:

1. **Wind chill below 0°F.** This is where Should I Run shows its extreme cold warning. Exposed skin is at real risk, and any problem (a twisted ankle, a wrong turn) gets serious quickly.
2. **Ice.** Freezing rain, sleet, freezing fog and refreezing puddles are more dangerous than the cold itself. Read [running on ice and black ice](/blog/running-on-ice-black-ice/) for how to spot them.
3. **Strong gusts.** Gusts above 30 mph push the app toward an easy day. Combined with deep cold, they make a hard session a bad idea.
4. **Hard workouts in deep cold.** If you need to hit specific paces, a wind chill in the single digits will cost you 6–8%. A treadmill gives you a cleaner session.

If you do go out in extreme cold, run short loops close to home so you're never far from warmth.

## Frostbite Risk on Exposed Skin

Frostbite happens fastest on skin that's both exposed and in the wind: cheeks, nose, ears, chin and fingers. The NWS wind chill chart gives approximate frostbite times for exposed skin:

- **Around -20°F wind chill:** frostbite possible in about 30 minutes
- **Around -35°F wind chill:** about 10 minutes
- **Around -50°F wind chill and colder:** about 5 minutes or less

Those times assume bare skin, but a long run can easily last 30 minutes or more, so the first threshold is relevant to a lot of winter runners. Warning signs include numbness, tingling, and skin that looks pale, white or waxy. If you notice them, cover up and head inside.

## Protecting Lungs, Hands and Face

### Lungs

Cold, dry air can make your throat and airways feel raw, especially during hard efforts. A neck gaiter or balaclava pulled over your mouth warms and humidifies the air a little before you breathe it. Keep the effort easy on very cold days. If you have asthma or another breathing condition, talk to your doctor about cold-weather running.

### Hands

Hands get cold early and stay cold. Mittens are warmer than gloves because your fingers share heat. A thin liner glove under a mitten lets you adjust as you warm up. Should I Run moves from light gloves to warm gloves to insulated gloves as the dress-for temperature drops.

### Face and Ears

Below a dress-for temperature of 25°F, the app recommends a balaclava. Below 15°F, it recommends full coverage. A thin layer of a barrier balm on cheeks and nose can help with wind burn. Cover ears early, because they lose heat quickly.

For exactly which layers to wear at each temperature, see [what to wear running by temperature](/blog/what-to-wear-running-by-temperature/).

## FAQ

### What temperature is too cold to run outside?

There's no single cutoff, but a wind chill below 0°F is where Should I Run warns you to consider the treadmill. Above that, cold is mostly a clothing problem.

### Is running in the cold bad for your lungs?

For most healthy runners, cold air feels uncomfortable but isn't harmful at normal efforts. Covering your mouth and keeping the effort easy helps. People with asthma or exercise-induced breathing problems should get advice from a doctor.

### Should I run slower in cold weather?

Yes, a little. At a wind chill of 20–29°F, expect to run about 2–4% slower. Below 10°F, expect 6% or more.

### Do I need to warm up longer in the cold?

A gradual start helps. Begin with a few minutes of easy running before you pick up the pace, so muscles warm up before you ask them for speed.

## Bottom Line

It's rarely too cold to run if you dress for the wind chill, cover your skin, and accept a slower pace. Wind chill below 0°F, ice, or strong gusts are the real reasons to move inside.
