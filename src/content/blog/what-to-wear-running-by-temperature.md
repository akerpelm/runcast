To know what to wear running by temperature, take the feels-like temperature and add 15–25°F for the heat your body makes while running, then dress for that number. An easy run at 40°F should be dressed like a 55°F day, and a hard interval session at the same temperature like a 65°F day.

That one adjustment fixes most clothing mistakes. The rest of this guide is the full chart we use in [Should I Run](/), how effort level shifts it, and the weather modifiers that add layers on top.

## Step 1: Find Your Dress-For Temperature

Standing still, 40°F feels cold. Ten minutes into a run, it doesn't. Your muscles produce a lot of heat, so the temperature you *feel* while running is well above the forecast.

Should I Run calculates a **dress-for temperature** like this:

**Dress-for temp = feels-like temp + effort offset**

The feels-like (apparent) temperature already accounts for wind and humidity. The effort offset depends on how hard you're running:

| Effort | Offset |
|---|---|
| Easy | +15°F |
| Endurance | +17°F |
| Tempo | +20°F |
| Threshold | +22°F |
| Interval | +25°F |

If you always end up carrying your jacket home, turn on the **"I run hot"** setting. It adds another +5°F to every effort.

## Step 2: The Running Clothing Chart

For any single temperature, the [temperature guides](/what-to-wear-running/) break this down by effort, wind and humidity, from [0°F](/what-to-wear-running/0-degrees/) to [90°F](/what-to-wear-running/90-degrees/).

Once you have your dress-for temperature, find your row:

| Dress-For Temp | Bottom | Top | Accessories |
|---|---|---|---|
| Above 75°F | Split shorts | Singlet or sports bra | None |
| 65–75°F | Shorts | T-shirt or singlet | None |
| 55–64°F | Shorts | T-shirt or light long sleeve | Arm sleeves (optional) |
| 45–54°F | Shorts or half tights | Long-sleeve tech tee | Light gloves (optional), headband |
| 35–44°F | Running tights | Long-sleeve base layer + half-zip | Gloves, headband or ear cover |
| 25–34°F | Running tights | Base layer + midweight jacket (or fleece + wind shell) | Warm gloves, beanie, neck gaiter |
| 15–24°F | Insulated tights | Thermal base + insulated jacket | Insulated gloves, balaclava, neck gaiter |
| Below 15°F | Insulated tights + wind pants | Base + mid + wind shell | Full coverage, insulated gloves, balaclava |

Remember that these are *dress-for* temperatures, not forecast temperatures. For an easy run, subtract 15°F to get the forecast range each row covers. The 45–54°F row, for example, is what you wear on an easy run when it feels like 30–39°F outside.

## How One Temperature Changes by Effort

Here's why effort matters so much. Take a calm 30°F morning, where the feels-like temperature is close to the air temperature:

| Effort | Dress-For Temp | Tier | What to Wear |
|---|---|---|---|
| Easy | 45°F | 45–54°F | Shorts or half tights, long-sleeve tech tee, headband |
| Endurance | 47°F | 45–54°F | Shorts or half tights, long-sleeve tech tee, headband |
| Tempo | 50°F | 45–54°F | Shorts or half tights, long-sleeve tech tee, headband |
| Threshold | 52°F | 45–54°F | Shorts or half tights, long-sleeve tech tee, headband |
| Interval | 55°F | 55–64°F | Shorts, T-shirt or light long sleeve |

The same morning with a 10 mph wind is different. The NWS wind chill drops to about 21°F, which puts an easy run at a dress-for temperature of 36°F: running tights, a base layer and half-zip, gloves and ear cover. A breeze can move you a whole tier or more. For the full walk-through at this temperature, see [what to wear running in 30 degree weather](/blog/what-to-wear-running-30-degrees/).

## Step 3: Add Weather Modifiers

The chart handles temperature. Three conditions add a layer on top:

- **Wind over 15 mph:** add a wind-resistant layer. Wind strips heat through breathable fabrics, and a thin shell blocks most of it.
- **Active precipitation:** add a water-resistant layer.
- **Rain chance of 50% or more:** bring a light rain shell, even if it's dry when you leave.

Wind has its own guide: [running in the wind](/blog/running-in-wind/).

## Why Effort Matters More Than You Think

Most "dress 15–20 degrees warmer" advice assumes a steady easy run. That works until you do a workout. Interval sessions produce bursts of heat, and if you've dressed for an easy jog you'll be overheated by the second rep.

The flip side is warming up. Easy miles before a workout are colder than the workout itself. A good approach on cold workout days:

1. Dress for the workout effort, not the warm-up.
2. Add one removable layer (a vest or light jacket) for the warm-up.
3. Drop it somewhere on your loop before the hard part starts.

## Common Mistakes

1. **Dressing for the forecast, not the run.** If you're comfortable standing outside before you start, you're overdressed.
2. **Ignoring wind.** The thermometer can say 40°F while the wind chill says low 30s. Check the feels-like number.
3. **Too much on the legs.** Your legs make a lot of heat. Many runners are comfortable in shorts well below what feels reasonable.
4. **Forgetting hands and ears.** Extremities get cold first. Light gloves and a headband cost nothing to carry.
5. **Cotton.** It holds sweat and gets cold. Choose synthetic or merino layers at every tier.

## FAQ

### What temperature is too cold for shorts when running?

By the chart, shorts stop being the main recommendation when your dress-for temperature drops below 45°F. For an easy run, that's a feels-like temperature around 30°F. Some runners keep wearing shorts lower than that, and that's fine if you're comfortable.

### Should I dress differently for a race?

Yes. Races are usually run at tempo effort or harder, so use the higher offsets. Most runners overdress on race day because they're cold in the start corral. A throwaway layer solves that.

### Does the chart use the actual temperature or the feels-like temperature?

The feels-like temperature, which includes the effect of wind and humidity. On a windy day it can be several degrees colder than the thermometer.

### What does the "I run hot" setting do?

It adds +5°F to your dress-for temperature at every effort level, which usually moves you about half a tier lighter.

## Bottom Line

Find your dress-for temperature (feels-like plus your effort offset), match it to the chart, then add a shell for wind or rain. Getting the base number right is most of the work.
